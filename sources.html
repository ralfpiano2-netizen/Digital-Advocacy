Sources

Sources

Every number on this site, where it came from, and how we used it. Numbers that come from models are labeled “estimate,” and each local study names the place it covers.

Scope

How wide?National: the Philippines only. The main estimate covers e-commerce sales across the whole country, not one platform or city (Huan et al., 2023). Local studies (Metro Manila, Cavite, Camarines Sur) are shown as examples, not as national numbers.
How many countries?One. India and Vietnam appear only on What other countries did, to compare laws. Global studies are used only as context and are labeled global.
Which e-commerce?Online orders of physical goods shipped to homes as parcels: marketplace apps like Shopee, Lazada and TikTok Shop, and online sellers who ship through couriers. These platforms are examples, not the only ones. One limit: the national estimate starts from total e-commerce sales, so it may include some orders outside our scope; the study does not break it down by type.
What packaging?The single-use plastic added for shipping: poly mailers, bubble wrap, air pillows, packing tape and plastic labels.
Where does it begin and end?It begins when an order is packed for shipping, and we follow it to where it ends up: a dumpsite, a fire, a drain, a river, the sea, and back to our food. Then the next order starts the loop again.
What can it affect?Land and dumpsites, the air (when burned), drains and flooding, rivers, Manila Bay and the sea, marine animals, fish and shellfish, and the people who eat them.
Which parts of society?Shoppers and households, online sellers (mostly small businesses), platforms, couriers, waste pickers and junk shops, local government units (LGUs) and the waste system, and national lawmakers.
When?2022 is the base year, the latest year with a national estimate, with projections to 2030. Most sources are from 2020 onward; older ones are flagged.
What our research focuses onFour questions: how much parcel plastic there is, where it goes after the bin, what it harms, and which laws cover it and where they fall short.

Limitations: what we couldn’t measure

  • No official count. No agency publishes how many parcels move through the Philippines each year, or how much plastic is in each one. We found no public packaging data from Shopee, Lazada or TikTok Shop (Greenpeace Philippines, 2021).
  • One national model. The 11,790 t comes from a single study whose authors call it a rough estimate that hasn’t been validated. Its 15.47 g per piece comes from a 2013 Canadian study (Huan et al., 2023).
  • Mixed packaging. The model starts from total e-commerce sales, so it can’t separate parcel wrap from a product’s own packaging, or online retail from other orders like food delivery.
  • Once thrown away, it’s mixed in. Numbers for drains, rivers, the sea and fish cover all garbage or all plastic. No study we found separates parcel plastic after it leaves the bin, so we label those numbers as context.
  • No local leak rate. We found no Philippine measurement of how much plastic leaks from dumpsites into the sea; the estimate we show is global and uses 2010 data (Jambeck et al., 2015).
  • Small, local studies. The local studies are small or survey-based: 30 homes in Metro Manila (21 by week 4), an online survey of 243 people in Dasmariñas, Cavite, and 30 frequent-shopper homes in Pasacao, Camarines Sur. They show patterns, not national totals (Leon et al., 2022; Sadie et al., 2024).
  • Where-it-goes shares are national averages. The 72% / 88% / 7% / 5% split comes from the country’s general waste data, not from tracking parcel plastic. In the model, “loss” means the plastic became waste after use, not that it went missing from the waste system (Huan et al., 2023).
  • Burning isn’t in the model. The study mentions a 7% burning rate but leaves it out of its model, so the ≈590 t burned is our own calculation (7% × 8,490 t).
  • Fish guts, not fish meat. The bangus study looked at the gut, not the part we usually eat (Philippine Daily Inquirer, 2022).
  • Our 2025 figure is our own calculation, not a study. No published 2025 study exists yet. We reused the study’s method with 2025 sales for Shopee, Lazada and TikTok Shop only (Htet, 2026). The ₱310 order value is a simple average of the three platforms, not weighted by orders. It keeps the study’s assumptions (2 pieces per order, 15.47 g each, the 72/88/7/5 split) and has not been checked by other researchers. That’s why 11,790 t (2022) stays our main number.
  • Data stops at 2022. That is the latest national estimate. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) recovery reports for 2023–2024 are company-reported and third-party audited, so we can’t check them ourselves, and they don’t show how much of the recovered plastic was parcel film (Cabalza, 2026).
  • Other countries are context only. The Vietnam details come from a business briefing, not the decree’s own text, and every country’s size and system differ, so their rules can’t simply be copied (Vietnam Briefing, 2024).

How we adjusted some numbers

Claims we used, but not in the exact form we found them.

Claim we foundWhat we use instead
A measured % of plastic leaking from Philippine dumpsites/landfills to the seaNo Philippine study measures this. We show the global 15–40% range from Jambeck et al. (2015) and label it an assumption based on 2010 data.
“Eco-design and green packaging standards remain voluntary”We could not find this wording in our sources. The World Bank (2021) says no national design-for-recycling standard exists, so we say that instead.
“Fish is Filipinos' top animal protein, 40 kg per person a year”Outdated. Recent estimates range from 14.3 to 26.6 kg depending on the source and method.
Tape and label glue as a main recycling barrier (World Bank)Not found in the World Bank report; we use WWF-Philippines (2020, p. 31) on low-value film instead.
“The Philippines is the world's top ocean polluter, 36%”Mixes two different measures; we use the river model's own wording instead.
“2.66% of low-density polyethylene (LDPE)/high-density polyethylene (HDPE) waste”The math gives 0.27%; we use tonnes.
“+375% by 2030”True only for the order-twice-as-often scenario; we show the full range.
