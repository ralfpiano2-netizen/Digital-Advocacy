Station 1 · Add to cart · Voluntary platform measure

Platform packaging programs and rules

Relevance to e-commerce packaging

Directly relevant Written for e-commerce packaging: the only measure on this list that is.

It cuts both ways. The pilots remove some plastic, but Shopee’s binding guide requires a plastic outer layer on every boxed item.

Current status

  • 2021: 14,625 people petitioned Shopee and Lazada to “reveal, reduce, and redesign” their packaging. According to Greenpeace, Lazada only acknowledged the letter and Shopee did not respond (Greenpeace Philippines, 2021).
  • 2021–2022: Lazada’s “greener parcels” used paper fillers for some partner brands packed in its own warehouses (adobo Magazine, 2022). Unilever and Lazada announced FSC-certified boxes and shredded-paper fillers in Vietnam, Indonesia and the Philippines (Unilever Philippines, 2022). Johnson & Johnson and aCommerce piloted paper packaging for Philippine orders (Scoop, 2022).
  • 2026: Shopee Philippines’ Unified Packaging Guidelines bind all sellers; non-compliance “may result in parcel and claims rejection” (Shopee Philippines, 2026).
  • Published results for the Philippines: Not disclosed

How it works

Two separate mechanisms operate at this station.

  1. Voluntary pilots. Selected brands packed in the platform’s warehouses replace plastic fillers with shredded paper (Marketing-Interactive, 2021). Most small sellers pack their own orders and are not included.
  2. Binding packing rules. Shopee’s guide prescribes materials by item type (Shopee Philippines, 2026):
Item group (Shopee’s examples)InsideOutside
Non-breakable (T-shirts, towels)No fillers; pouch or boxIf boxed: one layer of black stretch film or black bubble wrap
Potentially breakable (shoes, bags, snacks)Two tight layers of bubble wrap per item, “or other eco-friendly options”One layer of black stretch film or black bubble wrap
Spillable, fragile, hazardous, bulkyTwo tight layers of bubble wrap per itemOne layer of black bubble wrap

Some provisions limit plastic: pouches must be “snug and tucked” with “minimal excess space,” and paper honeycomb filler and gum tape are permitted (Shopee Philippines, 2026). The rules exist to prevent damage and theft.

Results

180,000plastic bottles’ worth: a projected saving from a Lazada pilot in Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam, not a result (Marketing-Interactive, 2021)
3+plastic layers on a boxed, breakable item packed to Shopee’s rules (our reading of the guide)

The Johnson & Johnson and aCommerce pilot aimed to save 106,684 m² of plastic Projected; no result was published (Scoop, 2022). Abroad, Amazon replaced 95% of its plastic air pillows with paper in North America, about 15 billion a year Self-reported (Packaging Insights, 2024).

Assessment

Effective?Unknown
Sustainable?Weak

Platforms can change how orders are packed at scale, as Amazon’s switch shows Self-reported (Packaging Insights, 2024). In the Philippines, however, the pilots are voluntary and limited to a few brands, and their results are Not disclosed. The one rule that binds every Shopee seller adds plastic: by our reading, a boxed breakable item carries at least three plastic layers plus tape, against the national model’s assumption of two pieces per order (Huan et al., 2023).

Implication: Packaging decisions already sit with the platform, but the rule is private and currently increases plastic.

Why it should be prioritized: A single change to one platform’s packing rule reaches every seller on that platform at once.

Station 2 · Seller · National law

Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) Act

Relevance to e-commerce packaging

Indirectly relevant Covers all plastic packaging of large companies.

Small sellers are exempt, and the law does not name online platforms or marketplaces. Mailers added by small sellers therefore have no clearly obliged party.

Current status

  • Lapsed into law on July 23, 2022 (Cabalza, 2026). Implementing rules: DAO 2023-02, January 24, 2023 (Parkinson, 2026).
  • Of about 2,130 businesses believed covered, 1,017 had registered by December 2025 Reported (Vyas et al., 2026).
  • Fine schedules were approved in April 2026; rules for accrediting auditors were still unfinished (Vyas et al., 2026).
  • In August 2026, DENR issued show-cause letters to 155 firms suspected of non-compliance Reported (Cabalza, 2026; Parkinson, 2026). A show-cause letter is not a finding of violation.
  • The 2026 target is 60%; audits and notices are ongoing (GMA News, 2026).

How it works

  1. Register. Each covered firm (assets above ₱100 million) files an EPR program (Cabato, 2023).
  2. Recover or offset. 20% (2023), 40% (2024), 50% (2025), 60% (2026), 70% (2027), 80% from 2028 (Cabalza, 2026).
  3. Count. “Recovery” includes recycling, upcycling and co-processing, that is, burning plastic as fuel in cement kilns. The law counts these equally (Parkinson, 2026; Vyas et al., 2026).
  4. Audit. A third-party auditor verifies the figures; the auditor is paid by the company audited (Republic Act No. 11898, 2022; Vyas et al., 2026).
  5. Penalize. ₱5 million to ₱20 million, permit suspension for repeat offenders, or twice the cost of recovering the shortfall if higher (Parkinson, 2026).

Results

Share of recovered plastic recycled versus burned in cement kilns: Not disclosed (Vyas et al., 2026). Share that was e-commerce mailers or bubble wrap: Not disclosed

Assessment

Effective?Partly
Sustainable?Yes

Covered companies exceeded the 2024 target by about 20 points for rigid and 16 points for flexible plastic Audited Our math, which shows that firms can be held to account for their packaging. Three factors limit the figure: each company declares its own footprint, part of the “recovery” may have been burned, and no data show less plastic per order. Critics add that the law sets collection targets but does little to reduce plastic use (Sabillo, 2024).

Implication: The law holds large brands accountable, but its recovery rate does not distinguish recycling from burning and does not reach small sellers.

Why it should be prioritized: It is the only law that already makes companies pay for their packaging. Extending it to platforms (Proposal 2) builds on a system that already runs.

Station 4 · Unboxing · City program

Quezon City Trash to Cashback

Relevance to e-commerce packaging

Directly relevant It accepts “online shopping plastic packages,” and its FAQ names bubble wrap.

It operates in one city and collects plastic after use; it does not prevent plastic from being added.

Current status

  • Launched at Quezon City Hall in March 2021 and extended to barangay drop-off points the same year, when online shopping packaging was added to accepted items (Quezon City Government, 2021).
  • Results are reported as total kilograms and transactions (see Results). Share that was e-commerce plastic packaging: Not disclosed. Share recycled after collection: Not disclosed

How it works

  1. Register with BeepXTRA, the city’s points partner (Quezon City Government, 2026).
  2. Bring and weigh. Single-use plastics must weigh at least 3 kg to earn a point; other recyclables at least 1 kg (Quezon City Government, 2022).
  3. Redeem points at the Trash to Cashback Mart or for partner deliveries (Quezon City Government, 2022).

Requirements: collection points, staff, a points partner and funds for rewards. Program cost: Not disclosed

Results

869,722.93 kgcollected, 2021 to Feb 2026, all accepted materials
94,080transactions
≈ 9.2 kgper transaction on average (869,722.93 ÷ 94,080)

Before-and-after comparison: No baseline. Collected is not the same as recycled.

Assessment

Effective?Yes, for collection
Sustainable?Likely

It is the clearest working example of a program that takes e-commerce plastic packaging back from households, supported by five years of figures. Its threshold is high for e-commerce plastic film: 3 kg of single-use plastic equals roughly 97 orders’ worth at about 31 g per order Our math, a figure based on the national model’s assumption and offered only as an illustration (Huan et al., 2023).

Implication: Take-back works when participants receive something in return, but the program covers one city and measures collection, not reduction.

Why it should be prioritized: It is the only Philippine program found that explicitly accepts e-commerce plastic packaging and rewards households for returning it. Other local governments can replicate it.

Station 5 · Trash can · National law

Ecological Solid Waste Management Act: segregation and MRFs

Relevance to e-commerce packaging

Indirectly relevant Covers all solid waste.

It reaches e-commerce plastic packaging only after disposal. Sorted film often has no buyer and may still end up as residual waste.

Current status

  • Approved January 26, 2001 (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001).
  • 2021: 11,637 MRFs were operating Audited (Commission on Audit, 2023). 2025: 12,864 MRFs; 343 sanitary landfills served 748 LGUs; 1,416 of 1,592 cities and municipalities (89%) had compliant 10-year plans Reported (Context.ph, 2025). Barangay coverage is charted under Results.
  • “None of the policy’s time-bound goals have been met”; LGUs received “little to no financial support,” and fines remain “minimal” (European Commission & GIZ, 2025).
  • 2026: EMB-NCR joined the national commission’s validation of Metro Manila plans, including Manila’s 2025–2034 and Valenzuela’s 2023–2032 plans, on 18–19 February, inspecting MRFs, collection vehicles and segregation bins (Environmental Management Bureau–National Capital Region, 2026).
  • National waste diversion rate: Not found

How it works

  1. Segregate biodegradable, recyclable and residual waste at home.
  2. Collect. Barangays bring recyclables to their MRF. Some cities refuse mixed waste; Manila’s Environmental Code (Ordinance No. 8371, 2014) provides that garbage not segregated in approved containers “shall not be collected” (Article V, Section 8 A[c]), enforced as “No Segregation, No Collection” (City Council of Manila, 2014; Journal News, 2023; Manila Public Information Office, 2025). Manila’s school-based MRFs and “Tapon-to-Ipon” bins were also noted as best practices in the 2026 validation (Environmental Management Bureau–National Capital Region, 2026; Manila Public Information Office, 2025).
  3. Plan. Each LGU follows a 10-year solid waste plan; the law required 25% diversion within five years. Plans are validated by the national commission, with EMB-NCR joining the 2026 validation in Metro Manila (Environmental Management Bureau–National Capital Region, 2026; Republic Act No. 9003, 2001).
  4. Prohibit. Open dumps must close and open burning is banned (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001).

Results

Assessment

Effective?Low, but improving
Sustainable?Depends on the LGU

Coverage rose from 39.05% (2021) to nearly half (2025). At 1.8 to 2.6 points a year, full coverage is roughly 20 to 30 years away Our math Projected. The law contains no provision on e-commerce packaging, and where an MRF exists, sorted film still requires a buyer.

Implication: Republic Act No. 9003 (RA 9003) is the collection system every other measure depends on, and it remains about half-built, with no outlet for film.

Why it should be prioritized: Take-back programs and EPR both depend on a place to bring the plastic, so strengthening this system supports every other measure.

Station 6 · Drain · Local ordinance

City plastic ordinances (Quezon City bag fee)

Relevance to e-commerce packaging

Indirectly relevant Written for shopping bags.

None of the ordinances found covers e-commerce mailers or bubble wrap, and an online order crosses city boundaries that a single ordinance cannot follow.

Current status

  • As of 2019, 489 cities and municipalities (30%) had a plastics policy Reported (Climate Change Commission, n.d.); their effectiveness had not been assessed (WWF-Philippines et al., 2020).
  • Quezon City passed its Plastic Bag Reduction Ordinance (SP-2140) in 2012 and incorporated it into its Environment Code (SP-2350) in 2014 (C40 Cities, n.d.).
  • The Quezon City data cover 2012 to 2018, earlier than this site’s 2020 cutoff; they are used because this is the best-studied case.

How it works

  1. Charge at the counter. Large stores charge ₱2 per plastic bag at least 15 microns thick (C40 Cities, n.d.).
  2. Hold the fee in a green fund. Stores keep the fees for environmental programs that the city reviews and approves (Braganza, 2017).
  3. Monitor. The city’s environment office inspects stores.

Results

6,269,278plastic bags recovered in Quezon City by the end of 2017 Self-reported(C40 Cities, n.d.)
≈ ₱207MQuezon City green fund from the ₱2 bag fee, 2012–2016
0ordinances found that cover e-commerce mailers or bubble wrap

The study concludes the ordinance “may have somewhat reduced” household plastic (Braganza, 2017). Size of the reduction: No baseline

Assessment

Effective?Bags: yes; online orders: no
Sustainable?Yes

The green fund reached about ₱207 million from 2012 to 2016 Reported (Braganza, 2017), which shows that stores kept collecting the fee and that it can pay for programs. E-commerce packaging remains outside its reach: a bag is bought at a counter in one city, while an online order is packed in one city and discarded in another.

Implication: Local governments can run a small, visible charge for years, but no ordinance found reaches online orders.

Why it should be prioritized: It shows that a small, visible rule at the point of sale can run citywide for years, the same lever behind Proposal 1.
