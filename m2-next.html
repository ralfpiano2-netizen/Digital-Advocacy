Milestone 2 · What’s Missing?

The measures exist. E-commerce packaging is still not covered.

Conclusions, remaining gaps and six proposals.

Our answerThe gapsDeveloping the proposalsProposalsPriorityMeasuring success

Our answer

No single measure is enough. Three actions are needed together.

The Philippines has ways to collect e-commerce plastic packaging, but no rule prevents it from being added.

Before packing

Reduce

The plastic never used needs no management. This stage has the least action, and the platform’s own rule adds plastic here.

After unboxing

Return

Take-back that rewards households, as in Quezon City, linked to a buyer that accepts film.

At every stage

Assign responsibility

Name who is accountable, platforms included, and require them to report how much packaging they use and where it goes.

On the waste hierarchy, Reduce is the top rung (prevention), Return covers reuse and recycling, and Assign responsibility makes every rung measurable.

Existing rules were written for plastic in general, not for e-commerce packaging. They reach it only after it is thrown away, if at all.

Implication: the strongest call is directed at Congress and Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR), where one rule reaches every online order, followed by the platforms, which already set the packing rules.

What this does not claim

  • That the existing measures achieve nothing: Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), materials recovery facilities (MRFs) and Trash to Cashback all move tonnes of plastic.
  • That single-use plastic is always the problem: it is inexpensive, protects goods and is often the option for low-income buyers.
  • That the scores are measurements: they are judgments tied to published evidence.

Measures abroad do not create the gaps; they show which fixes have been tried (Other countries). The gaps then pass through the filters and become the proposals.

  • Station 1 · Add to cartNo say at checkout

    Buyers have no say in how an order is packed: the seller packs it, following the platform’s rule. No checkout option to ask for less packaging was found.

  • Station 2 · PackingPacking rule adds plastic

    Sellers must follow the platform’s packing guide, and Shopee’s binding guide requires plastic layers on boxed items.

  • Station 2 · SellerSmall sellers and platforms outside Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

    Small sellers, who pack most orders, are exempt, and platforms are not named.

  • Station 4 · UnboxingTake-back in one city only

    Quezon City’s program is the only one found, and it requires 3 kg of plastic for one point.

  • Station 5 · Trash canNo outlet for sorted film

    About half of barangays have materials recovery facility (MRF) service, and sorted film often has no buyer.

  • Station 5B · BurnedBurning counted as recovery

    EPR recovery includes co-processing in cement kilns, and the split is not published.

  • All stationsNo baseline

    No one reports e-commerce plastic packaging separately, so reduction cannot be measured.

Beyond the trash can

From the river to the sea, the fish and the plate (Stations 7 to 10 in Milestone 1), no measure is aimed at e-commerce plastic packaging. What exists there are general cleanups that remove all kinds of waste after it has leaked, such as the waterway cleanup in Manila shown here.

Cleanups treat the result, not the source. Each of the seven gaps above lies upstream, where the plastic is added, discarded or counted.

Developing the proposals

From many ideas to six proposals.

The proposals were developed in four steps: list every idea, sort the ideas by cause, filter them, then check what stakeholders have said about them.

1 · Brainstorming

Every idea that could reduce e-commerce plastic packaging waste was listed first, without judging whether it would work. The web shows the ideas as they came up, grouped only loosely by theme.

28 ideas around six loose themes, listed without judging them, including impractical ones. Solid lines join an idea to the theme it came up under; dashed lines join ideas that build on each other. Sources: the group’s Evaluating Solutions worksheets and the team’s idea sessions.

2 · Mind mapping and grouping

The ideas were then sorted by the root cause they address, meaning the underlying reason the waste exists. Four root causes were drawn from the group’s Concept Map Worksheet in Milestone 1:

Root cause 1 · Frictionless ordering
Mega sales, free shipping and one-item orders make buying quick and cheap, so more small packages are sent.
Root cause 2 · Protective overpacking
Sellers add layers of plastic to avoid damage complaints, and the platform’s packing guide requires some of them.
Root cause 3 · Fragmented accountability
Platforms and small sellers decide how the package is made, but no law assigns either of them responsibility for it.
Root cause 4 · Low-value film
Thin plastic film has no resale value, so collectors do not pick it up and recovery counts it even when it is burned.

Similar ideas were merged into one candidate. Each card shows the root cause it addresses, the gap it answers and the ideas merged into it.

Root-cause check

The gaps come from the route, so a cause that sits outside any one station could be missed. Each root cause was therefore checked for a candidate. The first check found that root cause 1 had none that reduces the number of deliveries; the packaging charge was developed from that finding and became Proposal 6.

Two ideas from outside the gap list were then checked against root causes 1 and 2:

  • Ship it in its own box: items that arrive from the supplier in a sturdy box go out in that box, with no extra wrap. At JD Logistics in China, shipments sent without repackaging rose from about 5% to 25%, removing more than 1 billion pieces of secondary packaging in 2024 Self-reported (China Daily, 2025), and Taiwan’s rules exempt goods shipped in their original box (Ho, 2025). Added to Proposal 1.
  • Combine a buyer’s orders into one delivery: published results Not found. It is built into Proposal 6 as a way to avoid the charge, and its effect is tracked there.

The gap with no outlet for sorted film (Station 5) is covered by two proposals: Proposal 3 requires a film buyer before launch, and Proposal 6 funds film collection.

Proposals

Six proposals.

Proposals 1 to 5 follow the route of an online order; Proposal 6 came from the root-cause check. These are proposals, not existing programs. The tab on each shows the station and the gap it answers.

1Station 1 · Add to cart

Answers: No say at checkout · Packing rule adds plastic

Proposal 1. Make minimal packaging the default

Shopee already enforces its packing guide by rejecting non-compliant packages (Shopee Philippines, 2026). Revising that guide, or making “minimal packaging” the pre-selected checkout option, would reach every order at once.

Defaults change behavior. Grab made cutlery opt-in in 2019, and 898 million cutlery sets, about 8,088 tonnes, were avoided in 2022 across Southeast Asia Self-reported (Grab Holdings, 2023). Zomato made “no cutlery required” the default, and 74% of orders then went without cutlery Self-reported (Business Today, 2022). Both examples come from food delivery, not online retail.

Limits written for online orders already exist abroad. Taiwan caps packaging weight for online retailers; six months after its mandatory rules began, average packaging per product was 12% lower than a year earlier Reported; South Korea limits delivery packaging to 50% empty space and one layer of wrap (Ho, 2025). The EU’s 50% empty-space rule starts in 2030 at the earliest (Fieldfisher, 2025). (Other countries)

10% of orders → about 589 tonnes of plastic avoided per year

Isang Parcel projection, not a measurement: 381 million orders (2022) × share × 15.47 g per piece (Huan et al., 2023). At 5%: ≈ 295 t; at 25%: ≈ 1,474 t. Assumes items still arrive undamaged and nothing replaces the plastic.

How it would work

  1. A platform pre-selects “minimal packaging” at checkout, with an opt-out for fragile items.
  2. The packing guide allows a pouch only for non-breakable items and makes paper filler the default for breakable ones.
  3. Items that arrive from the supplier in a sturdy box ship in that box, with no extra wrap.
  4. One product category is piloted, recording plastic grams per order, damage and returns.
  5. Results are published; the default is extended if damage does not rise.
Who acts
Platforms; Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) or Congress to require it
Who bears the cost
Platforms and sellers (pilot costs, possible damage claims)
Who gains
Shoppers and local government units (LGUs) (less waste to collect)

What this does not claim: That less plastic never means more damage; the pilot must track damaged and returned orders.

2Station 2 · Seller

Answers: Small sellers and platforms outside Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

Proposal 2. Bring platforms under the EPR Act

India named online platforms and marketplaces as “brand owners” under its plastic packaging rules in February 2022 (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, 2022). Amazon India has since declared 17,643 tonnes and Flipkart 16,639 tonnes of plastic Self-reported (Navya, 2024).

In the Philippines, the House approved a single-use plastics bill (HB 9147) on July 28, 2021, by 190–0–1, but it did not become law (Inquirer.net, 2021). A similar bill filed on December 15, 2025 would cover online stores (House of Representatives, 2025). The Internet Transactions Act of 2023 (Republic Act No. 11967, 2023), in full effect since June 2025, already makes platforms share liability with sellers, though for consumer protection rather than waste (Newsbytes.PH, 2025).

How it would work

  1. Congress amends Republic Act No. 11898 (RA 11898) to name e-commerce platforms as obliged parties for the packaging of orders they process.
  2. Department of Environment and Natural Resources (DENR) issues rules on how platform-handled packaging is counted.
  3. Platforms register, report and meet recovery targets through their own or collective programs.
  4. Costs may be shared with large sellers through platform fees.
Who acts
Congress, DENR
Who bears the cost
Platforms (fees, programs), partly passed on to large sellers
Who gains
LGUs (funded collection) and small sellers (a shared system)

What this does not claim: That India’s results are proven: they are company declarations, and no Indian agency yet tracks e-commerce packaging waste.

3Station 4 · Unboxing

Answers: Take-back in one city only

Proposal 3. Bring take-back points closer to home

Extend the Trash to Cashback model to barangays and courier hubs, with a lower threshold for e-commerce plastic film; single-use plastic currently requires 3 kg for one point (Quezon City Government, 2022).

Keep reuse local. A 2024 study found a returnable mailer had higher impact than a single-use one in 9 of 10 categories, even after 40 reuses, because of long return trips; a local rental box performed better in 6 of 10 (Park et al., 2024). The full evidence on reusable mailers is under Looked at, set aside.

How it would work

  1. A barangay or courier hub hosts a drop-off point, modeled on Trash to Cashback.
  2. The points threshold for film is set below 3 kg.
  3. A buyer or processor that accepts film is contracted before launch.
  4. Kilograms received, sent to recyclers and actually recycled are recorded.
Who acts
Couriers, platforms and LGUs
Who bears the cost
LGUs and platforms (drop-off points, rewards)
Who gains
Households (rewards) and recyclers (supply of film)

What this does not claim: That collected means recycled; a take-back needs a buyer or processor at the end.

4Station 5B · Burned

Answers: Burning counted as recovery

Proposal 4. Require EPR reports to separate recycling from burning

DENR does not publish how much “recovered” plastic was recycled and how much was burned as fuel. Credits for co-processing have sold for about a fifth of the price of recycling credits, so burning is the cheaper way to meet the target (Vyas et al., 2026).

How it would work

  1. DENR revises the EPR reporting format to separate recycling, upcycling and co-processing.
  2. Third-party auditors verify each pathway.
  3. DENR publishes pathway totals every year.
Who acts
DENR, NSWMC
Who bears the cost
Large companies (reporting)
Who gains
The public and DENR (a true recycling rate)

What this does not claim: That co-processing is always wrong: it keeps plastic out of dumps and rivers, but it is not recycling and has its own air-quality impact.

5All stations

Answers: No baseline

Proposal 5. Measure first: require packaging reports

Singapore requires companies with annual turnover above S$10 million to report packaging weight by material and form, and to file reduce-reuse-recycle plans (National Environment Agency, n.d.). Germany keeps one central register of producers and their packaging volumes by material (Central Agency Packaging Register, n.d.). Taiwan tracks average packaging weight per product, which lets it report change from year to year (Ho, 2025). A Philippine version could begin with the three largest platforms.

How it would work

  1. DENR requires the largest platforms to report packaging by weight, material and form.
  2. Reports include grams of plastic per order.
  3. Data are published yearly and provide the baseline for the other proposals.
Who acts
DENR, platforms
Who bears the cost
Platforms (reporting)
Who gains
DENR, researchers and every other proposal (a baseline)

What this does not claim: That reporting alone reduces plastic; it provides the baseline every other proposal requires.

6Station 1 · Add to cart

Answers: No say at checkout · No outlet for sorted film

Proposal 6. Charge for e-commerce plastic packaging at checkout

This proposal came from the root-cause check, not from a station gap. Mega sales, free shipping and one-item orders make each extra delivery feel free. A visible charge for e-commerce plastic packaging gives buyers a reason to combine orders, and the money pays for collecting the film.

The model already exists in the Philippines. Quezon City’s ₱2 bag fee has run since 2012 and built a green fund of about ₱207 million by 2016 Reported; the study found it “may have somewhat reduced” household plastic, with no measured percentage (Braganza, 2017). House Bill 6853, filed in December 2025, counts online stores as commercial establishments and proposes a charge of at least ₱5 per single-use plastic item for take-out and delivery (House of Representatives, 2025).

Effect: not estimated. Philippine data on how online shoppers respond to a packaging charge: Not found

How it would work

  1. Checkout shows a packaging line: a small charge for each order packed in single-use plastic.
  2. No charge when the buyer combines orders into one delivery or picks minimal packaging (Proposal 1).
  3. Orders below a set value are exempt, so the charge does not fall hardest on low-income buyers.
  4. The money goes to a fund for film collection and take-back points (Proposal 3), with yearly public reports, as Quezon City does with its green fund.
Who acts
Congress (through HB 6853 or an amendment); platforms collect the charge
Who bears the cost
Shoppers who choose plastic packaging; platforms (checkout changes)
Who gains
LGUs and recyclers (funded film collection); shoppers who combine orders or pick minimal packaging pay nothing

What this does not claim: That a charge alone cuts e-commerce plastic packaging: the bag-fee study found only that it “may have somewhat reduced” use. The right amount is not known; ₱5 is the figure in the House bill, not a tested value.

Measuring success

Measure first, then judge.

Collecting more is not the same as wasting less, so four levels are tracked separately.

LevelQuestionWhat to measureStarting point
1 · CoverageWho is covered?Share of online orders sold by platforms or sellers under a packaging ruleNone: no rule names platforms
2 · ComplianceWho reports?Share of covered firms filing complete, audited reportsExtended Producer Responsibility (EPR): 1,017 of about 2,130 likely-covered firms registered by December 2025
3 · OperationsWhat was collected, and where did it go?Tonnes collected, recycled and burned, reported separatelySplit not published
4 · OutcomesIs there less plastic per order?Grams of plastic per order, before and afterAbout 31 g per order, a model assumption

Pilot protocol

  1. Stage 0Confirm partners and safety controls.
  2. Stage 1Measure a baseline: grams of plastic per order, by product type.
  3. Stage 2Test right-sized packaging on comparable products, monitoring damage, returns and cost.
  4. Stage 3If a film processor is contracted, record film accepted, rejected, dispatched and recycled.
  5. Stage 4Compare against the baseline and report the uncertainty.

Readiness conditions

A proposal is not ready to launch unless all four are met, however well it scores:

  • A confirmed buyer or processor for collected film
  • A documented budget and who pays
  • A measured baseline
  • Damage and safety controls

Pilot stages and readiness conditions adapted from the group’s Milestone 2 research report.

A proposal should be dropped or revised if orders arrive damaged more often, if small sellers’ packing costs rise sharply, or if collected film has nowhere to go.
