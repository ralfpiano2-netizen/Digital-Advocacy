Method

Each score is tied to published evidence.

These are the measures currently in place in the Philippines that act on e-commerce packaging, directly or indirectly.

Each measure is scored from 1 to 5 on five criteria that Ekvall sets for judging a method: feasible, accurate, comprehensible, inspiring and robust (Ekvall, 2019). A sixth criterion is added for this site, relevance: whether the measure addresses e-commerce packaging directly, or only as part of all plastic.

Ekvall’s five criteria judge whether a measure is sound in general; they do not ask whether it addresses this site’s topic. Relevance asks that question, and it counts twice so that a measure that is strong in general but ignores e-commerce packaging cannot rank first on general merit alone. Score = (sum of the five + 2 × relevance) ÷ 7. Feasibility alone cannot rank the five, because four of them score 4 on it; the other criteria decide the order. Every score is tied to a published source: implementation records, audits, participation figures, survey results and stakeholder statements.

Waste hierarchy

Most measures act near the bottom of the ladder.

The waste hierarchy ranks options from best to worst: prevention, preparing for reuse, recycling, other recovery such as burning for energy, and disposal.

The order comes from the European Union’s Waste Framework Directive, which sets it as the “priority order” for waste law and policy (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2008). Here, each of the five Philippine measures is placed on the rung where it acts on e-commerce plastic packaging.

  1. PreventionLess packaging used in the first place
    Platform pilotspaper fillers for a few brands; the binding packing rule still adds plasticCity bag feeshopping bags only, not online orders
  2. Preparing for reuseThe same packaging used again
    No Philippine measure found
  3. RecyclingPlastic made into new material
    QC Trash to Cashbackcollects film; share recycled not disclosedRA 9003 + MRFssorts recyclables; film often has no buyerEPR Actthe recycling part of “recovery”
  4. Other recoveryBurned for energy
    EPR Actco-processing in cement kilns counts as recovery; share not disclosed
  5. DisposalLandfill
    RA 9003sanitary landfills for residual waste

Implication: three of the five measures act only after the packaging is thrown away, and the Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) Act counts burning as recovery. The two top rungs, which keep plastic from becoming waste at all, have almost nothing aimed at online orders. The proposals on What’s Missing? start there.

Other countries

Benchmarks from abroad.

Four of the six were written for e-commerce or delivery packaging. Their figures measure different things, so the comparison is of methods, not percentages.

Why these are not scored. The six criteria on this page are scored from Philippine evidence: whether a measure runs here, whether Filipinos took part, whether it is in Philippine law. Measures abroad cannot be judged on that evidence, so a score would compare unlike things. Each is checked on the same three questions instead and used as evidence for a proposal (filter “Has worked elsewhere” on What’s Missing?).

TaiwanLimits by weight

Packaging limits for online retail

Voluntary guidelines for online-shopping packaging began in August 2019. Mandatory rules took effect in July 2023: plastic packaging must contain at least 25% recycled material, PVC is banned, and large firms must cut average packaging weight by 25% (2024), 30% (2025) and 35% (2026) against 2019, or ship 2%, 8.5% and 15% of online orders in reusable packaging. Fines reach NT$150,000 (Ho, 2025).

Written for e-commerce packaging?

Yes It covers online retailers and platforms, including foreign ones selling to Taiwan.

Evidence of results

Under the voluntary guidelines, average packaging per product fell 23%, to about 0.25 kg (2019–2022), but the targets were missed. Six months into the mandatory rules it was 12% lower than a year earlier, at 0.22 kg Reported; inspections were still limited at that point (Ho, 2025).

What the Philippines would need

A rule that applies to platforms and sellers, and a baseline in grams of packaging per order.

Supports Proposal 1: minimal packaging by default → Proposal 5: packaging reports →

South KoreaSpace and layer caps

At most half empty, one layer of wrap

Since April 2024, delivery packaging may be at most 50% empty space, with fillers counted as empty space, and may use only one layer of disposable wrapping. The rule covers about 1.32 million distributors and platforms; firms with sales below 50 billion won are excluded. Fines reach 3 million won (Ho, 2025).

Written for e-commerce packaging?

Yes Written for delivery packaging.

Evidence of results

Not found. Penalties were waived for two years from March 2024, with full enforcement due in 2026; the September 2025 source says results “remain to be seen” (Ho, 2025).

What the Philippines would need

A packing standard that every seller follows; the platform’s own packing guide is the fastest route.

Supports Proposal 1: minimal packaging by default →

European UnionDesign standard

A cap on empty space

Regulation (EU) 2025/40 applies from 12 August 2026. From 1 January 2030, or later if its implementing rules are delayed, e-commerce packaging may be at most 50% empty space, with bubble wrap and air cushions counted as empty space; at least 40% of transport packaging must be reusable from 2030 (Fieldfisher, 2025).

Written for e-commerce packaging?

Yes E-commerce packaging is named.

Evidence of results

None yet. The empty-space rule starts in 2030 at the earliest.

What the Philippines would need

A national packing standard, which the Philippines does not have.

Supports Proposal 1: minimal packaging by default →

IndiaAccountability

Platforms count as brand owners

Since February 2022, India’s plastic packaging rules list online platforms and marketplaces among the “brand owners” that must recover their packaging; micro and small enterprises are excluded (Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change, 2022).

Written for e-commerce packaging?

Yes Platforms are named.

Evidence of results

Amazon India 17,643 tonnes and Flipkart 16,639 tonnes of plastic Self-reported. No agency tracks e-commerce packaging waste separately (Navya, 2024).

What the Philippines would need

An amendment to Republic Act No. 11898 (RA 11898) naming platforms.

Companies with annual turnover above S$10 million report packaging by weight, material and form, and file reduce-reuse-recycle plans (National Environment Agency, n.d.).

GermanyOne register

Register every package

Producers report their packaging volumes by material type, in kilograms, to a central register (LUCID) (Central Agency Packaging Register, n.d.).

Written for e-commerce packaging?

No It covers all packaging.

Evidence of results

17.9 million tonnes of packaging waste in 2023, 69.4% recycled; all packaging, not e-commerce packaging only Reported (Umweltbundesamt, n.d.).

What the Philippines would need

One public register of who places which packaging on the market.

Tried elsewhere, but the conditions matter.

Two alternatives often suggested for online orders, checked against the evidence.

Reusable mailers

Sent back and used again

A sturdy mailer or box that the buyer returns so it can carry another order.

In the Philippines

Advocates asked Lazada and Shopee in 2020 to add reuse systems (Philstar.com, 2020). Philippine program: Not found

Evidence

A 2024 study found that a returnable mailer had higher environmental impact than a single-use one in 9 of 10 categories, even after 40 reuses, because of long return trips; a local rental box did better in 6 of 10 (Park et al., 2024). Taiwan lets large firms meet their target by shipping a share of online orders in reusable packaging, rising from 2% (2024) to 15% (2026) (Ho, 2025). A Singapore pilot found that take-up depended on consumer education (video below).

Our assessment

Works only under conditions Short return trips and a high return rate. Kept in its local form inside Proposal 3; dropped as a nationwide system.

Biodegradable mailers

Sold as compostable

Mailers made to break down after use, sold as an alternative to plastic film.

In the Philippines

Advocates in 2020 pointed to compostable packaging used by some local brands (Philstar.com, 2020). How widely it is used: Not found

Evidence

Study showing a lower total impact in Philippine conditions: Not found. Like film, it still needs collection that keeps it separate, and only about half of barangays have materials recovery facility (MRF) service (see RA 9003: Results).

Our assessment

Not carried forward It could shift the harm instead of removing it, so it fails the “Does not shift the harm” filter on What’s Missing?.
